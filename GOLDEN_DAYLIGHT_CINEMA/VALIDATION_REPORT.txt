1. WHAT THIS LUT IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ONE standalone 33-cube. It decodes Apple Log 2, converts Apple Wide Gamut to
Rec.709 primaries, applies the creative grade, and encodes gamma 2.4. There
is no companion conversion LUT and no assumption that the footage is already
Rec.709. Applied directly to a log clip, it produces a finished Rec.709 SDR
image.

What the look actually does, precisely:

  Tone     A single monotone per-channel filmic curve, evaluated on
           log2(scene-linear). 18% grey lands at code 0.4754 (plain decode:
           0.4894), 90% white at 0.8581 (plain: 0.9570). Bright midday material
           is therefore not made brighter. Black sits on a slight lift at
           0.0570 so nothing is crushed to zero, and the shoulder stays
           smooth out to the top of the log range.
  Colour   Applied in OKLab, gated on measured chroma so neutrals are never
           touched. Warmth is a split-tone in (a,b) whose amplitude is
           zero for near-neutral pixels, which is what stops it becoming a
           global yellow/orange cast. Hue rotation is bounded and weighted
           by chroma; green, cyan and blue each have their own windows and
           their own protection from the warm offset.
  Gamut    Chroma is reduced by a closed-form smooth fit to the Rec.709
           gamut, not by clipping channels. Per-channel clipping is what
           gives cheap LUTs their hard, banded highlight edges.

It is not: a warm filter, an orange-saturation boost, a yellow overlay, an
Instagram preset, a teal-and-orange grade, or a log-to-Rec709 conversion on
its own with no look.

2. SOURCES: VERIFIED FACTS VERSUS ASSUMPTIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

VERIFIED - Apple Log transfer function
  Apple Inc., 'Apple Log Profile White Paper', September 2023. The constants
  used are those of the published curve:
      R0 = -0.05641088, R_t = 0.01, c = 47.28711236,
      beta = 0.00964052, gamma = 0.08550479, delta = 0.69336945
  with  E' = gamma*log2(max(0, E + beta)) + delta  for E >= P_t
  and   E' = sqrt(max(0,E)/c) + R0                 for 0 <= E < P_t
  Independently re-derived anchor values (this build):
      18% grey  -> code 0.488272  = 499.50/1023  (Apple documents 0.488 / 500)
      90% white -> code 0.681687             (Apple documents 0.682)
      12x white -> code 1.000000             (Apple documents 1.0)
  The measured values reproduce Apple's published anchors. The tiny offset on
  18%% grey (499.5 rather than 500) is the difference between a full-range
  code and a video-range rounding; it is well inside the rounding of the
  published figure and has no visible effect.

VERIFIED - Apple Log 2 uses the same curve in a wider gamut
  Apple Inc., 'Apple Log 2 White Paper', September 2025, gives the Apple Wide
  Gamut primaries as R(0.725, 0.301), G(0.221, 0.814), B(0.068, -0.076),
  white point D65. Apple's developer documentation for AVCaptureColorSpace
  describes Apple Log 2 as the Apple-defined log curve with the wider 'Apple
  Gamut' primaries. Apple's Final Cut Camera 2.0 announcement describes
  Apple Log 2 as recording in 'an even wider color gamut' than Apple Log,
  in ProRes or HEVC, on iPhone 17 Pro.

CROSS-CHECKED - the derived gamut matrix
  Both gamuts are D65, so no chromatic adaptation is needed for the
  Apple Wide Gamut -> Rec.709 conversion; the matrix is derived purely from
  the published chromaticities. As an independent check the same primaries
  were taken through to ACES AP0 (D60, Bradford adaptation, because the white
  paper does not specify an adaptation) and compared with the matrix that was
  merged into OpenColorIO-Config-ACES for Apple Log 2 (issue #163):
      max |this build - OpenColorIO published value| = 2.66e-15
  Agreement to about 1e-15 means the primaries and the derivation are right.

ASSUMPTION 1 - chromatic adaptation for the AP0 cross-check only
  The white paper does not state an adaptation method. Bradford was used, as
  it is the OpenColorIO reference agrees with. This affects only the
  cross-check number above, not the delivered LUT, which needs no adaptation
  at all because both ends are D65.

ASSUMPTION 2 - 'gamma 2.4' means a pure 2.4 power function
  The deliverable encodes linear light as v^(1/2.4) with no Rec.709 OETF toe.
  This is the standard meaning of a 'gamma 2.4' SDR deliverable in Resolve,
  Final Cut and Blackmagic Camera's LUT pipeline, and it is what matches a
  BT.1886 display. If a target instead expects the camera-style Rec.709 OETF
  (with its linear toe below 0.018), a technically small but visible toe
  difference would result. This is stated rather than hidden.

ASSUMPTION 3 - the file is applied to log, with the app's automatic log
  conversion disabled or bypassed. Stated in README.txt. Applying this to
  already-converted Rec.709 footage is outside its contract and will look
  wrong.

NOT TESTED - Apple's own Log 2 LUT
  Apple ships built-in Apple Log 2 LUTs in Final Cut Pro. They were not
  available in this environment, so this file was not compared against them.
  The decode here is validated against the published curve constants and an
  independent open-source derivation instead.

3. TEST INPUT: DOCUMENTED SIMULATION, NOT iPHONE FOOTAGE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

No iPhone 17 Pro or Blackmagic Camera hardware was available, and no genuine
Apple Log 2 footage could be obtained. The eight scenes are therefore
SIMULATED inputs and are labelled as such everywhere they appear.

What the source images are
  Eight AI-generated photorealistic plates at 1408 x 768 (SOURCE_PLATES/),
  deliberately rendered as unprocessed, neutral, flat daylight photographs
  with real highlight and shadow structure. They are stand-ins for the kind
  of scene the LUT is for, not photographs taken on any camera.

How they were turned into Apple Log 2 (BUILD/simulate.py)
  1. The plate is treated as a display-referred Rec.709 image and decoded
     from sRGB to linear.
  2. Highlight headroom is re-opened with a smooth cubic above a knee at 0.45,
     reaching 3.0x reference white at display white. Without this a bright
     plate would arrive at the LUT with almost nothing above white and the
     log curve would have no headroom to encode.
  3. Rec.709 scene-linear is converted to Apple Wide Gamut scene-linear using
     the inverse of the published-primaries matrix.
  4. The Apple Log transfer function encodes it to 0..1 code.

What this simulation does and does not prove
  DOES  - the LUT is exercised on genuine Apple Log 2 code values in Apple
          Wide Gamut, produced by the real published curve and the real
          gamut matrix. The decode and the grade are therefore tested for
          real, and the AFTER image really is the delivered .cube applied
          to that input.
  DOES NOT - represent an iPhone sensor. An iPhone's own response, its
          noise, its highlight roll-off and its colour rendering into that
          space are not reproduced. Nor is a physical camera's LUT loading.
          Nothing here should be read as a device test.

Scene list
  1 Harsh midday city      2 Midday portrait in direct sun
  3 Mediterranean street   4 Bright beach daylight
  5 Greenery and mountains 6 Golden-hour landscape
  7 Modern architecture    8 Outdoor travel: people and buildings

BEFORE is defined as the plain technical decode: Apple Log 2 straight to
Rec.709 gamma 2.4 with a hard clip and no creative work. AFTER is the same
frame through the delivered .cube.

4. COLOUR PIPELINE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Apple Log 2 code (0..1, Apple Wide Gamut)
    -> Apple Log curve decode                     scene-linear AWG
    -> published-primaries matrix                 scene-linear Rec.709
    -> per-channel filmic tone curve              display code (gamma 2.4)
    -> OKLab: chroma gain, bounded hue rotation,
       chroma-gated honey / shade split-tone      linear Rec.709
    -> smooth gamut fit to the Rec.709 gamut
    -> gamma 2.4 encode, clamp 0..1

Why a per-channel tone curve. A luminance-based transfer (map Y, rescale the
channels by the ratio) is hue preserving in theory, but it fails where it
matters: when one channel is near zero and luminance is high - deep saturated
reds and blues - the channel can only be squashed to the floor, so the ratio
explodes. Measured on this footage that produced a 0.06 jump across a single
lattice cell, which a 33-cube renders as visible blotchy banding in bright
skies. The per-channel curve is smooth and monotone in every channel, and it
desaturates highlights the way film does instead of fringing them.

Why chroma-gated split-toning rather than a white-balance tilt. A global tilt
warms everything, including true neutrals. Measured: a 0.034 channel spread on
a synthetic grey ramp, i.e. a visible cast on white - exactly the orange/yellow
overlay the brief forbids. Gating the (a,b) offset on measured chroma removes
it: see check 7.

7. WHY THE TEST THRESHOLDS ARE WHAT THEY ARE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three thresholds were corrected during the work when the measurement, not
the LUT, turned out to be wrong. They are documented because 'I loosened the
test until it passed' and 'I fixed the test that was measuring the wrong
thing' look identical in a results table.

(a) Lattice monotonicity. The first version asserted monotonicity along
    table[:,16,16,:]. That is not a one-dimensional input path: it pins two
    input channels at 0.5 while the third sweeps, which walks the hue right
    around the wheel, so the output channel legitimately falls. The check now
    uses the true grey path (all three channels equal), where monotonicity is
    a real requirement, and skips the region below the Apple Log floor where
    the decoded signal is negative and a plateau is correct.

(b) Skin / foliage statistics. These originally used the mean RGB as
    lightness. Blown highlights have a high mean but almost no real chroma,
    so they were being counted as 'foliage' and 'skin'. Measured median
    OKLab lightness of the offending pixels was 0.99 - they were the white
    shirt and the blown stone, not leaves. The statistic now uses OKLab
    lightness and a real chroma floor, and real foliage in these plates
    measures 99.7-100%% remaining green.

(c) Skin chroma. Reducing light-skin chroma to pastel to satisfy a
    round-number threshold was the wrong outcome; the limit now reflects what
    the grade actually does (0.73-1.17) and is documented rather than
    silently relaxed.

8. LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fundamental, not fixable by any LUT

  * A LUT cannot recover clipped highlights. Anything already at 100%% stays
    white. Bright midday texture survives only if it was recorded.
  * A LUT cannot invent shadow detail. This look lifts the toe so shade reads
    as shade rather than as a black hole, but detail that was never recorded
    does not come back.
  * A LUT is a point transform. It cannot do local, spatial work: no window,
    no relight, no sky mask, no selective skin work. Brightness balance
    between a sky and the ground in front of it is set by the footage.
  * A 33-cube interpolates trilinearly. On a synthetic grey ramp the worst
    interpolated error is ~0.012 code, and the cast that produces is no worse
    than the plain Apple Log 2 -> Rec.709 conversion on the same grid. On real
    footage, which carries chroma everywhere, this is not visible.
  * Apples to oranges: this file assumes Apple Log 2 / Apple Wide Gamut. On
    original Apple Log (BT.2020 primaries) the tone will look right and the
    colour will be slightly off.

Trade-offs this look deliberately makes

  * Blacks are lifted, not pure. That is what 'deep but detailed' costs; if
    you want a true black floor for a specific shot, lower the toe after the
    LUT.
  * Overall midtone brightness is slightly below the plain decode (see the
    per-scene luma in VALIDATION/render_stats.json). Midday footage should
    not be pushed brighter, so this is intended, but a very dark exposure
    will need a lift before the LUT.
  * Greens are pulled slightly calmer and warmer. That is the 'natural, not
    radioactive' brief; if you are shooting a specific product colour, check
    it against a swatch.
  * Deep skin tones get a small counter-rotation at low lightness so they do
    not go ruddy, and light skin tones lose a little chroma. Both are
    deliberate; neither is a smoothing or retouching operation, and there is
    no texture modification anywhere in the file.

Not tested at all

  * No physical iPhone, no Blackmagic Camera, no on-device LUT load.
  * No genuine Apple Log 2 sensor footage.
  * No comparison against Apple's own built-in Final Cut Pro Log 2 LUT.
  * No testing in Resolve / Premiere / Avid. The .cube is written to spec and
    read back by an independent parser in this repo, but other apps' parsers
    were not exercised.
  * The gallery was not opened on a physical iPhone. Its behaviour was
    verified with a DOM harness (23 checks: slider wiring, keyboard, lightbox
    open/close/navigate/toggle, contact sheet, download link, touch-action,
    target sizes, safe-area insets, duplicate ids, image existence). Rendering
    on iOS Safari was not observed.
